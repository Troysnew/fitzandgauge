Why did "fireproof" iron buildings fail in the 1871 Chicago fire?

Iron can't burn, so Gauge thinks iron buildings must be fireproof. Chicago's 1871 fire showed what heat does to iron.

▶ Premieres Sat Dec 5 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. Gerald R. Larson, architectural historian, The Architecture Professor (2020): iron buildings thought to be totally fireproof were destroyed in the 1871 fire as easily as others. Iron is non-combustible, but cast iron lost rigidity at a relatively low rise in temperature, and iron columns expanded faster than the masonry around them, so they buckled or pushed floors out of line.

    thearchitectureprofessor.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Chicago History Museum, Olivia Mahoney, "From Ashes to Terra-Cotta" (2017): stone, iron and brick were thought to be fireproof, but they were no match for the fire's intense heat. Chicago companies went on to make terra-cotta tiles for fireproofing interior frames.

    chicagohistory.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. The Skyscraper Museum, New York (2022): claims of "fireproof building" were regularly disproved, and Chicago's Great Fire in 1871 revealed iron's vulnerability to collapse. Lightweight terra cotta let architects combine ceramic's resistance to fire with iron framing.

    skyscraper.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. National Institute of Standards and Technology, Metallurgy Division (2005), on modern structural steel, iron's successor: by 600 °C most structural steels have lost more than half their strength, so steel in buildings is insulated to keep it cool in a fire.

    metallurgy.nist.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. Chicago Historical Society and Northwestern University, "The Great Chicago Fire and the Web of Memory": the Chicago Tribune was burned out of its 1869 "fireproof" building at Dearborn and Madison, one example of a "fireproof" building that did not survive.

    greatchicagofire.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  6. Gerald R. Larson, The Architecture Professor (2020): Peter B. Wight and William Drake patented an iron column encased in red oak in 1874. In their fire test the encased column survived with only slight charring while the other two columns failed.

    thearchitectureprofessor.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Gerald R. Larson, The Architecture Professor (2020): Wight then switched to porous terra cotta, and his first terra-cotta-encased columns went into the six-story Chicago Club Building late in 1875.

    thearchitectureprofessor.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  8. Mary Schons, National Geographic Society (updated 2023): terra-cotta clay emerged as a popular and effective building material after the fire, and by the mid-1880s terra cotta tiling made Chicago one of the most fireproof cities in the nation.

    education.nationalgeographic.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  9. National Park Service, Preservation Brief 27, "The Maintenance and Repair of Architectural Cast Iron": from 1840 James Bogardus promoted cast iron above all for its fire-resistant qualities, showing how widespread the belief in iron's fire safety was.

    nps.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗
