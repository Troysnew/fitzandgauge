Case File #3 · City Secrets · 13 min

Why did Chicago lift its streets and buildings?

Case Files: Chicago set out to lift a whole hotel, turned its river around, and dug a giant tunnel. Gauge keeps score in the city's long fight with its own water.

▶ Premieres Sun Nov 22 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. "The lifting of Chicago: sources", transcriptions of the Chicago Tribune and Press and Tribune of 1860-61 and other period accounts, on a private enthusiast's site: the Tremont House lift (about 5,000 screws, 500 men, a six-foot target, one foot on day one, the hotel staying open), the 1860 Lake Street block (about 6,000 screws, 600 men, quarter turns on a signal, 4 feet 8 inches in five days), and George Pullman's firm. Nearly every building-raising figure comes from this one paper, so the episode credits them to the Tribune.

    nike-of-samothrace.net ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Louis P. Cain, Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Water", read in an Internet Archive copy: the flat, low, muddy city, gravity sewers, raising the streets, the river reversal (including the "quietly" removed dam in January 1900), St. Louis's fears and the court case, the sharp drop in typhoid, and storms that could still force the gates open. Its account dates from about 2004, so the episode puts that last point in the past tense.

    encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Louis P. Cain, Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Sanitation in Chicago", read in an Internet Archive copy: Chicago as the first large American city to build a comprehensive sewer system, the street raising, the lake tunnel, the canal, and chlorine from 1916. It is by the same author as "Water", so the two count as one voice.

    encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. Ann Durkin Keating, Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Sanitary and Ship Canal", read in an Internet Archive copy. With the article below, it supports the 28-mile canal from the South Branch to Lockport, the start of digging in 1892, and the flow toward the Mississippi. The archive link redirects to a 2023 snapshot.

    encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Main Channel Construction", read in an Internet Archive copy: with the article above, it supports the canal's route and the 1892 start of digging.

    encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  6. Chicago Public Library, Chicago Sewers Collection: why the city couldn't drain, Chesbrough's hiring in 1855, his plan to raise the streets six to ten feet and lay the sewers on top, buildings jacked up to match, and the 1889 Sanitary District.

    chipublib.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Chicago Public Library, "1900 Flow of Chicago River Reversed", read in an Internet Archive copy because the original page is gone: the low ridge west of the city, about eight feet high (this source alone, so the episode credits it by name), the canal, and the typhoid that kept coming back.

    chipublib.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  8. Chicago Public Library, "Early Cholera Epidemics", a death table from city health reports, read in an Internet Archive copy because the original page is gone: the 1,424 cholera deaths of 1854, and no cholera deaths in the 1880s. This and the two entries above are all from the library.

    chipublib.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  9. American Society of Civil Engineers, "Ellis Sylvester Chesbrough": his move from Boston in 1855, downtown raised by about 10 feet, the jackscrews, and the lake tunnel.

    asce.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  10. Linda Hall Library, "Centuries of Civil Engineering: Water Supply": the earlier intakes within about 600 feet of shore (this source alone), the two-mile lake tunnel dug by hand from May 1864, the crews meeting in 1866, and water flowing in 1867. It explains that 1863 was the design year, not the start of digging.

    lindahall.org ↗

  11. Linda Hall Library, "Scientist of the Day: Ellis Chesbrough": the lake tunnel and crib, and the first water in March 1867.

    lindahall.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  12. WTTW (Chicago PBS), 10 That Changed America, public-health engineering (2020), read in an Internet Archive copy: the first comprehensively planned sewer system in the country (Hamburg's came earlier), workers turning jacks together, the lake tunnel, and storm waste still reaching the new intake.

    wttw.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  13. WTTW News (Sept 28, 2023), "How Do Chicago's Lake Michigan Water Cribs Work?", read in an Internet Archive copy: the hard digging conditions, and the two crews lining up to within inches. Accounts differ on the exact gap, so the episode gives no figure. The archive link redirects to the nearest snapshot.

    news.wttw.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  14. Cecil Adams, The Straight Dope, built on river historian Libby Hill's research: the real Aug 2, 1885 storm, about five and a half inches of rain in 19 hours, no cholera deaths reported that year, and typhoid only slightly above average. It debunks the myth that 90,000 people died, which nearly every debunking traces to Hill, so the episode credits her.

    straightdope.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  15. Chicago Tribune, "Corrections and clarifications" (Sept 29, 2005), read in an Internet Archive copy because the original page is gone: the paper corrected its own earlier stories, finding no 1885 epidemic in its archives or public-health records, and reported Hill calling it an urban legend. Where the myth started is still unknown.

    articles.chicagotribune.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  16. U.S. Supreme Court, Missouri v. Illinois and the Sanitary District of Chicago, 200 U.S. 496 (1906), Justice Holmes's opinion, via Cornell's Legal Information Institute: the canal's opening in January 1900, and the case dismissed because Missouri's proof fell far short of its claims.

    law.cornell.edu ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  17. Kari K. Steele, president of the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District (MWRD), Chicago Sun-Times op-ed hosted by the MWRD (Feb 4, 2025): the canal to Lockport and the January 1900 reversal. The MWRD pages below are from the same agency, so they count as one voice.

    mwrd.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  18. MWRD, "Tunnel and Reservoir Plan": Chicago's combined sewers, overflows on more than 100 days a year by the 1960s, the Deep Tunnel's 1972 choice, its build from 1975 to 2006, its size (about 110 miles, 150-300 feet down, up to 33 feet wide), and overflows down to about 50 days a year. The archived copy is older than the live page we read.

    mwrd.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  19. MWRD, "TARP @ 50" (Oct 31, 2022): the overflows, the plan chosen in 1972, the tunnels' length and depth, and the drop in overflow days.

    mwrd.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  20. MWRD, "MWRD issues update on Tunnel and Reservoir Plan" (Aug 15, 2025): McCook Reservoir's 10-billion-gallon finished size and its revised completion around 2032. Older MWRD pages still say 2029, which this update replaces.

    mwrd.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  21. MWRD, TARP Status Report as of Dec 31, 2021: the plan selected in 1972, the tunnels finished in 2006 and up to 33 feet wide, the Thornton Reservoir in a former limestone quarry, and McCook's capacity.

    mwrd.org ↗

  22. MWRD TARP fact sheet (2019, Spanish-language edition): the 50 alternatives considered, why separating every sewer was rejected, the overflow days, and the Thornton quarry. The alternatives and the reasons come from this source alone.

    mwrd.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  23. MWRD, "McCook TARP Reservoir Live Stream": the McCook Reservoir about 300 feet deep (this source alone).

    mwrd.org ↗

  24. Jesse Dukes, WBEZ Curious City (2016), "Why the 1992 Loop flood is the most Chicago story ever": the freight tunnels about 40 feet down, the 1991 piling work, the leak found in January 1992, the April 13, 1992 flood, an estimated 250 million gallons, the evacuation, and that nobody was hurt.

    wbez.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  25. WBEZ (2012), "The Great Loop Flood": the tunnels' depth, the piling work and the flood date. It gives a different month for the piling work, so the episode says only "1991".

    wbez.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  26. CBS Chicago (2022), 30th anniversary of the Great Chicago Flood: the leak reported by a cable TV crew and the delayed repair, the car-sized hole, an estimated 250 million gallons, the plugging with the Army Corps of Engineers, the closures, and water drained into the Deep Tunnel (this source alone).

    cbsnews.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  27. Building Design + Construction (2002, reposted 2010), "Ten years after, Chicago looks back on fluke flood": the contractor's drawings that didn't show the tunnels (this source alone), the damaged tunnel roof, the hole, and the flooded basements.

    bdcnetwork.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  28. "Chicago's Movable Bridges Preservation Plan", by TY Lin International and Sullivan Preservation for the Chicago Department of Transportation (dated 2015; this copy is a 2018 final draft hosted on a third party's site): the city-designed "Chicago type" bridge, the trunnion, and the count of 44 movable bridges, 33 of which could still open. Other years give other counts, so the episode ties the figure to this plan.

    derivativeworks.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  29. American Society of Civil Engineers, Historic Civil Engineering Landmark, Cortland Street Drawbridge: the city's bridge study, and Cortland Street (1902) as the first trunnion bascule bridge built in the United States.

    asce.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  30. Invention & Technology, Cortland Street Drawbridge landmark page: the counterweight behind the pivot and the 1902 bridge. Its text likely matches ASCE's, so it is not an independent check.

    inventionandtech.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  31. Jack McGuinn, Gear Technology (Sept/Oct 2007), "Chicago's Movable Bridges", quoting Dan Burke of the Chicago Department of Transportation: the Chicago type, the counterweight, and the seesaw balance that needs little power.

    ik.imagekit.io ↗

  32. WBEZ (2013), "Bridges that span the river and the decades": why the river's bridges have to move.

    wbez.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  33. Chicago Architecture Center, "Trunnion bascule bridge": the seesaw design that tips each half up.

    architecture.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗
