Why do big ships have a round bump under the front?

Big ships have a round bump under the waterline at the front. Gauge wants to cut it off. Why is it there?

▶ Premieres Sat Nov 28 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. Manuel Ventura, IST Lisbon lecture slides: the wave system generated by the bulb interferes with the ship's own wave system and reduces the bow wave. The bulb shape must be adjusted to the design conditions, and at low speeds the effect of the bulb is generally negative.

    boatdesign.net ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Wartsila Encyclopedia, a manufacturer: a bulb is designed to reduce wave making resistance by developing an independent wave system that reduces the total wave system. It has a negative effect on power consumption when only partly submerged or not designed properly.

    wartsila.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Iglesias Baniela and Platero Diaz, The Journal of Navigation (2008), peer-reviewed, abstract only: each bulb is generally tuned to the expected operating speed of the ship.

    cambridge.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. Marine Insight, a trade explainer: the bulb creates its own wave, and designers aim for destructive interference with the bow wave. At very low Froude numbers bulbous bows have been found to increase drag, and bulbs are not efficient at all service speeds.

    marineinsight.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. FORCE Technology, a Danish hydrodynamics test institute: ships have traditionally been optimised for one single design point, and ships with bulbous bows are sensitive to off-design operation. Maersk Line exchanged the bow on five 4,500 TEU vessels and five 8,500 vessels.

    forcetechnology.com ↗

  6. Ships Monthly (2013), quoting Maersk Maritime Technology's Steffen Hartvig Nielsen: large container ships are typically designed for speeds of 25 knots, but with slow steaming the bulbous bows are often out of shape and generate high resistance.

    shipsmonthly.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Ship & Bunker (2013): Maersk Line says bulbous bows must be designed for a particular speed range, and it was modifying the bows of five Boston-class ships designed for high speeds to suit slow steaming.

    shipandbunker.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  8. PortNews (Dec 2012), citing SinoShipNews: Maersk Line was upgrading the bulbous bows of 10 container ships at Qingdao Beihai to cut fuel use while slow steaming.

    en.portnews.ru ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  9. Officer of the Watch blog (2013), a weak corroborating source: the whole bulbous bow of Maersk Brownsville was replaced with one matched to the ship's actual speed profile.

    officerofthewatch.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗
