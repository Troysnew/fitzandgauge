Why do fingers wrinkle in water?

Fingers go wrinkly in the bath. Gauge thinks they soak up water and swell. The real cause starts with your nerves.

▶ Premieres Tue Dec 8 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. Kareklas, Nettle and Smulders, Newcastle University, Biology Letters (2013), peer-reviewed: participants moved submerged objects more quickly with wrinkled fingers than with unwrinkled ones, with no difference for dry objects. The authors see this as support for the idea that wrinkles may help with handling wet objects.

    eprints.ncl.ac.uk ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Nature News (2013), republished by BioEd Online, Baylor College of Medicine: the common assumption is that water passes into the outer skin and makes it swell, but it has been known since the 1930s that fingers don't wrinkle when their nerves are damaged. Wrinkling is caused by blood vessels narrowing beneath the skin, and in the Newcastle test people handled wet marbles faster with wrinkled fingers.

    bioedonline.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Haseleu, Omerbasic, Frenzel, Gross and Lewin, Max Delbrück Center, PLOS ONE (2014), peer-reviewed: skin wrinkling did not improve dexterity in handling wet objects. The authors review evidence that wrinkling depends on the nervous system and suggest it may be a byproduct of blood vessels narrowing.

    journals.plos.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. Max Delbrück Center for Molecular Medicine press release (2014): wrinkling is an involuntary reaction controlled by the autonomic nervous system, in which tiny blood vessels in the skin constrict after water exposure. Testing 40 volunteers, the team found no grip advantage and could not reproduce the 2013 Newcastle result.

    mdc-berlin.de ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. Hsieh and colleagues, Clinical Autonomic Research (2006), peer-reviewed: in 20 replanted fingers without working nerve supply, the skin did not wrinkle in water, while the matching normal fingers did. The authors conclude the nerve supply is important in water-immersion wrinkling.

    springermedizin.de ↗

  6. Danielle Gerhard, The Scientist (2023), quoting neurologist Einar Wilder-Smith: the water-soaking explanation is not the answer. After a long soak, blood vessels just below the skin constrict and pull the outer skin down into wrinkles, and fingers with nerve damage did not wrinkle.

    the-scientist.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Nathaniel Scharping, Discover (2017): when the nerves to the hand are cut, fingers stop wrinkling, and wrinkling happens as a result of constricting blood vessels. A 2011 paper proposed the "rain tread" grip idea, while a 2014 follow-up concluded wrinkles may simply be a by-product.

    discovermagazine.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗
