Why do old New York skyscrapers look like wedding cakes?

Old New York towers step back as they rise. Gauge wants to build straight up. Why the wedding-cake shape?

▶ Premieres Fri Dec 11 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. NYC Department of City Planning, Zoning Handbook: the Equitable Building, erected in 1915, rose without setbacks to its full height and cast a seven-acre shadow over neighboring buildings, prompting worry about more buildings like it. New York enacted the nation's first comprehensive zoning ordinance in 1916, which fostered tiered "wedding cake" buildings.

    nyc.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. City of New York, Building Zone Resolution (adopted July 25, 1916), the original law text: where a building covers no more than 25 percent of the lot above a given level, the part above that level is exempt from the height and setback limits.

    nyc.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Equitable Building designation report (1996): the building rises approximately 38 stories straight up from the lot line with no setbacks, contemporaries complained it cut off sunlight from its neighbors, and it became the prime example cited in the passage of New York's zoning law, the first in the country.

    s-media.nyc.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. The Skyscraper Museum, Impact of 1916 Zoning: the law aimed to preserve a measure of light and air on the streets and sidewalks. Above a maximum height, buildings had to step back within a set diagonal, and a tower of unlimited height could rise over 25 percent of the lot, giving the stepped-pyramid shape.

    skyscraper.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. The Skyscraper Museum, Downtown New York exhibition: the law was designed to protect some light and air for Manhattan's streets. Above a set height, a building had to step back within an "angle of light" plane drawn from the center of the street, and a tower of unlimited height was allowed over one-quarter of the site.

    old.skyscraper.org ↗

  6. Museum of the City of New York, The Greatest Grid: the 1916 law required buildings to step back as they rose to protect sunlight on the street and lower stories, and allowed a tower of unlimited height on 25 percent of the lot.

    thegreatestgrid.mcny.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗
