Why do tall buildings have revolving doors?

Revolving doors are slow. Gauge wants a normal door instead. Why do tall buildings use them?

▶ Premieres Fri Nov 20 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. Slate, "The Green Lantern" (2008, with a July 29, 2008 correction): in winter heated air rises and cold air enters at ground level. A revolving door is not airtight but reduces the pressure-difference effect; the MIT student study of Building E25 found 23% of visitors used the revolving doors and that a swing door let in up to eight times more air.

    slate.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. MIT News (April 3, 2009): MIT students' research indicates that if everyone used the revolving doors in Building E25, MIT would save almost $7,500 in natural gas a year and nearly 15 tons of CO2.

    news.mit.edu ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Consulting-Specifying Engineer (2020), by a mechanical engineer: warm air rises and the lowest floors become negatively pressurized, sucking in cold air. Revolving doors achieve the same result as vestibules, limiting the cold air to a small puff.

    csemag.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. GreenBuildingAdvisor (2010), quoting building scientist John Straube: warm air rises, so the top of a heated building is pressurized and the bottom depressurized. Early skyscrapers needed revolving doors because the front door could not be opened against the stack effect pressure.

    greenbuildingadvisor.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. Pro Remodeler (2017, no named author, used as a cross-check): the effect grows with building height and temperature difference, and a normal door at the base of a very tall building can be very hard to open. Many tall buildings have revolving doors at ground level.

    proremodeler.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  6. Lee et al. (2012), Indoor and Built Environment, peer-reviewed abstract: stack-effect drafts in high-rise buildings are a known problem, and revolving doors are commonly used to reduce them, mainly at the lobby entrance.

    pure.skku.edu ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Boon Edam, a revolving-door manufacturer (interested party, cross-check only): reports the 2006 MIT study in E25 with 23% usage, a swing door letting through as much as 8 times more air, and almost $7,500 in natural gas a year.

    boonedam.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗
