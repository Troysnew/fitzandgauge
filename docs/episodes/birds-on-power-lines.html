Why don't birds get shocked on power lines?

Birds sit on live wires all day. Gauge wants to give them rubber boots. Do they need them?

▶ Premieres Wed Nov 11 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. BBC Science Focus, physicist Robert Matthews of Aston University: it is voltage differences, not voltages themselves, that make electricity flow. A bird on a single line has no voltage difference, but touching another line at a different voltage would electrocute it, which is why companies space cables apart.

    sciencefocus.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. MIT School of Engineering "Ask an Engineer", an independent cross-check: on a single wire both feet are at the same electrical potential, so current does not pass through the bird. The danger comes from touching a second wire at a different potential, or the wire plus the grounded pole.

    engineering.mit.edu ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Indiana Connection, an electric co-op magazine (2017), a third cross-check: there is no voltage difference in a single wire, and a bird is shocked if its body links two points at different potentials, such as the ground or a second wire.

    indianaconnection.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. USGS National Wildlife Health Center (N. J. Thomas, 1999): birds are electrocuted when they bridge two energized parts, or an energized part and a grounded metal part, most commonly where conductors are closer together than the wingspan. Raptors are especially at risk.

    usgs.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service: electrocution happens when a bird touches two energized parts or an energized part and a grounded part. Older poles had narrow clearances, and avian-safe poles provide enough separation and cover exposed parts.

    fws.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  6. American Eagle Foundation, citing APLIC: most electrocutions are on medium-voltage distribution lines where a wingspan can bridge the spacing. Fixes include wider spacing, longer crossarms, and covering conductors and equipment.

    eagles.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Avian Power Line Interaction Committee, a utility-industry group that works with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and others on bird protection. Context only.

    aplic.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗
