How do geckos stick to walls?

Geckos walk up glass. Gauge is sure they use suction cups. Do they?

▶ Premieres Tue Nov 24 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. UC Berkeley press release (2000) on Autumn et al., Nature, quoting Robert Full and Kellar Autumn: suction is ruled out and geckos stick in a vacuum, and there are no glue glands. Setae each end in hundreds to thousands of spatulae, and van der Waals forces are the likely explanation.

    newsarchive.berkeley.edu ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Autumn et al., PNAS (2002), peer-reviewed: direct experimental evidence of dry adhesion of setae by van der Waals forces. Adhesion is about the same on water-repelling and water-attracting surfaces, so wet adhesion is rejected as necessary.

    pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  3. Scientific American (2003), with answers by Kellar Autumn and Kostya Novoselov: detachment starts when the angle between the hair and the surface goes beyond 30 degrees. Toe peeling lifts only a few setae at a time, like peeling tape at a shallow angle.

    scientificamerican.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. California Academy of Sciences blog: suction, glue, interlocking, electrostatic attraction and friction were proposed and disproven. Geckos hold on with van der Waals forces through setae split into spatulae, and peel their toes up, which changes the setae angle and frees the foot.

    calacademy.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. Hu et al., Journal of the Royal Society Interface (2012), peer-reviewed: before each step geckos peel their toe pads by curling the toes from a distal to a proximal direction, lifting the setae in sequence.

    pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  6. EarthDate (weaker supporting source): each seta splits into hundreds of branches with flat tips, and a weak force called van der Waals pulls the tips and the surface together. Geckos peel their toes every step.

    earthdate.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  7. Phys.org (2011) on University of Akron research: the paper states geckos do not use glue, though footprints leave a thin phospholipid residue and no water was found at the contact.

    phys.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  8. Stanford Magazine (2000), weaker supporting source: gecko feet stick by tiny hairs (setae), not sticky secretions or suction cups.

    stanfordmag.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗
