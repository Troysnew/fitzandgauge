Case File #1 · Buildings · 12 min

Why was this NYC skyscraper secretly repaired at night?

Case Files: a 59-story tower on stilts, a hidden weakness, and welders working through the night. Gauge has red string. Lots of it.

Transcript

Every word of the episode, exactly as it was fact-checked.

Fitz
In 1978, this tower's structural engineer found a hidden weakness. He believed a strong enough storm could break it.
Gauge
A fifty-nine-story skyscraper. Standing on four stilts, in the middle of each side.
Gauge
I've cracked it already. They ran out of money and forgot the corners.
Fitz
Not quite. The corners are open on purpose.
Gauge
And nobody noticed the weakness?
Fitz
The full story stayed hidden for almost seventeen years.
Gauge
A secret skyscraper? Get the red string. All of it.
Fitz
Gauge, why are you tied to the lamp?
Gauge
Everything's connected, Fitz. Especially me and this lamp.
Fitz
This is Citicorp Center in Manhattan, finished in 1977, more than nine hundred feet tall. Its engineer was William LeMessurier.
Fitz
The stilts come first. One corner of the block belonged to Saint Peter's Lutheran Church.
Gauge
Theory two. The corners didn't run out of money. The church took them.
Fitz
Half right. One corner. The church kept its corner, and got a brand new building on that corner, with the tower rising over it. So no column could stand there.
Fitz
The four big columns went to the middle of each side instead. The corners hang out about seventy-two feet, with nothing underneath.
Gauge
That's like a table with the legs in the wrong place.
Fitz
It would be, without the braces. Behind the glass are steel braces, eight stories tall, in upside-down V shapes. They steer the weight and the wind from the corners down into those four columns.
Gauge
Secret V shapes. Pinning it. Pinning it twice.
Fitz
The frame was light. About twenty-five thousand tons of steel, less than half the Empire State Building's.
Gauge
Light is good. Light is fast. Light is... wobbly?
Fitz
Exactly. A light tower sways more in the wind. So near the top, LeMessurier added something special.
Fitz
A concrete block of about four hundred tons, riding on a thin film of oil. When the tower sways one way, the block is pushed the other way.
Fitz
Think of a passenger on a swaying train who always leans the other way.
Fitz
It cuts the sway by up to about half. It's called a tuned mass damper, and it was one of the first in a skyscraper.
Gauge
A giant concrete block, surfing on oil. I love him. I'm naming him Kevin.
Fitz
Please don't name the damper. But remember this: it was built for comfort, so people wouldn't feel the building move. Not to hold it up. And it needed electricity to work.
Gauge
Comfort. Needs power. Kevin needs a plug. Pinning it.
Fitz
Hold onto that one. It matters later.
Gauge
Fitz, I pinned a sandwich. I don't know why. It felt like a clue.
Fitz
That's your lunch.
Fitz
Here's how LeMessurier told the story. In June 1978, his office got a phone call from an engineering student in New Jersey. The student's professor wanted to know why the columns weren't at the corners.
Gauge
The professor agrees with me. The corners. Forgot. Them.
Fitz
LeMessurier explained the columns were placed to handle wind from every direction, even wind hitting a corner.
Fitz
Engineers call that a quartering wind. It hits at an angle and pushes on two sides at once.
Gauge
So it's like getting hit by two snowballs at the same time, right on the elbow.
Fitz
That's... actually a decent picture of it.
Fitz
Back then, New York's building code only asked engineers to check winds that hit a side straight on. LeMessurier thought the tower would make a good lesson for his engineering class. So he checked corner winds himself.
Fitz
In some of the big V-shaped braces, a corner wind raised the force by about forty percent.
Gauge
Forty percent! Case closed. The braces were too weak. Somebody get me a tiny judge's hammer.
Fitz
No. The braces themselves were strong enough. The question was how they were joined together.
Fitz
On paper, the joints were welded, which is very strong. But that May, at a meeting about a different building, LeMessurier had learned something about this one.
Fitz
The joints hadn't been welded. They'd been bolted.
Gauge
Plot twist. Pinning it. In red. I'm out of red. I'm using spaghetti.
Fitz
Back in 1974, the steel company suggested bolts instead of welds, to save money. His firm's New York office agreed.
Gauge
So bolts are the villain?
Fitz
No, bolts are normal and safe. The problem was what these were designed to hold.
Fitz
They were sized for straight-on winds only. And the braces had been counted as parts of a truss, not as columns, which left out a safety margin the rules required. So there were fewer bolts than there should have been.
Gauge
How does forty percent turn into a problem for a bolt?
Fitz
Picture a tug of war. The wind pulls the joint apart. The building's own weight pushes it back together. The joint only has to hold the difference. And the weight never changes.
Gauge
So the weight is the big kid at the back of the rope who refuses to move.
Fitz
Exactly. So when the wind's pull grew by forty percent, the leftover pull on the joints grew far more. LeMessurier worked it out at about one hundred and sixty percent.
Gauge
The tug-of-war math is not on our side. I'd like to switch teams.
Fitz
The wind tunnel lab in Canada that had tested the tower re-ran the numbers. Real storms shake a building, so the loads could be higher still.
Fitz
The weakest joint was at the thirtieth floor. He estimated how often a storm strong enough to break it comes along.
Gauge
And? How often?
Fitz
With the damper off, about a one in sixteen chance in any year.
Gauge
One in sixteen. So it's safe for fifteen years? I did math.
Fitz
You did math wrong. It's a one in sixteen chance every single year. With the damper running, he estimated about one in fifty-five.
Gauge
Kevin! Kevin can save the day.
Fitz
Only if he has power. And a big storm can knock the power out.
Gauge
Of course it can. Kevin, I'm so sorry.
Fitz
LeMessurier later said, "I had information that nobody else in the world had." It was late July.
Gauge
What do you do with information like that?
Fitz
That's the next question. And it's a hard one.
Fitz
He could have kept quiet. Instead, he told his insurance company, the architect, and then the bank's leaders.
Gauge
Worst meeting ever. "Hi, it's your engineer. Quick question about your tower."
Fitz
Within days, there was a plan. Steel plates about two inches thick would be welded over more than two hundred bolted joints.
Gauge
Like a bandage. A very heavy bandage. A two-inch bandage.
Fitz
The welders worked at night, from about eight in the evening to four in the morning, seven days a week. Each joint was boxed in plywood, and by morning people came to work as usual.
Gauge
So it's a heist movie. Except they break in at night to add steel.
Fitz
That was the idea. Emergency generators kept the damper running, and weather experts watched for storms.
Gauge
Kevin got a generator. Good for Kevin.
Fitz
And quietly, the bank, the city, the police and the Red Cross planned how to evacuate the tower and the neighborhood around it, if a dangerous storm came.
Gauge
What did they tell the public?
Fitz
On August eighth, the bank announced the joints would be strengthened. It said the engineers had assured it there was no danger.
Gauge
"No danger." Pinning that one. With a question mark. And a smaller question mark.
Fitz
The next day, a strike shut the city's big newspapers for nearly three months. The real reason never made the front pages.
Gauge
Talk about timing.
Fitz
Then, at the start of September, a hurricane named Ella was moving north off the East Coast.
Gauge
Tell me it didn't get close. Fitz. Tell me.
Fitz
The storm records show Ella stayed hundreds of miles out over the ocean, then turned away.
Gauge
Thank you, Ella.
Fitz
The welders fixed the most critical joints first, and finished in October. LeMessurier estimated the tower could now handle a seven-hundred-year storm, even without the damper.
Gauge
From one in sixteen to one in seven hundred. I'd take that trade. Kevin can finally relax.
Fitz
Two million dollars was paid to the bank through LeMessurier's insurance, with no lawsuit. And then, the story went quiet.
Gauge
Almost seventeen years quiet? I can't keep a secret for seventeen seconds.
Fitz
And when it finally came out, the famous version wasn't quite what happened.
Fitz
In 1995, the writer Joe Morgenstern told the full story in The New Yorker. It became famous. But parts of the famous version don't hold up.
Gauge
Myth check. I made a stamp. It says "myth." It also says "Gauge," because I couldn't fit it all.
Fitz
Myth one: a Princeton student found the flaw.
Fitz
There was a Princeton student, Diane Hartley. Her 1978 senior thesis studied this tower, and her numbers suggested corner winds raised the stresses.
Fitz
She raised it with an engineer at his New York office, never with LeMessurier himself. She learned about the crisis years later, from a TV show.
Gauge
So was she the caller?
Fitz
Nobody knows for sure. Another student, Lee DeCarolis, later said he made the call. He says he was passing on his professor's questions about the columns.
Gauge
Two students, one phone call. I'm going to need more spaghetti.
Fitz
And neither student knew about the bolts. LeMessurier found that problem himself.
Gauge
So it's not "a student found it." It's "students asked good questions."
Fitz
Exactly. Myth two: the tower was about to fall down.
Gauge
That's the one I believed. I've had the stamp ready since the cold open.
Fitz
That one in sixteen was LeMessurier's estimate in 1978. Decades later, researchers at the U.S. government's measurement lab took another look, with modern methods.
Fitz
Using a similar building, they found that straight-on winds, not corner winds, were the bigger load. So the danger from corner winds may have been overestimated.
Gauge
Wait. So it was fine all along? I wasted so much spaghetti.
Fitz
We can't say that either. The bolts were still short on their safety margin. What we know is that the repair made the tower much stronger.
Gauge
Myth three. Kevin was holding the building up.
Fitz
No. It was built for comfort. In 1978 it was only a backup, and only while the power stayed on.
Gauge
Kevin is a comfort block. That's still a very important job.
Fitz
Today the tower is called six-oh-one Lexington Avenue. In 2016 it became an official New York City landmark, and it's still full of offices.
Fitz
Engineering students study it in ethics classes, and LeMessurier is widely praised for coming forward about his own firm's mistake.
Fitz
But hard questions remain. The public was told there was no danger. Should people have been told more?
Gauge
And which student really made that phone call?
Fitz
And how close did it really come? Honest answer: people still disagree.
Gauge
Final theory. They didn't forget the corners. The corners were the whole point.
Fitz
That's the first theory you've had that I'd pin.
Gauge
So the case is closed, but the questions are still open.
Gauge
Also, I've known this secret for a whole episode and told no one. New record.
Fitz
You're telling everyone right now.
