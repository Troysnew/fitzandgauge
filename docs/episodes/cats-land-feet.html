How do cats land on their feet?

A falling cat flips right side up in midair. Gauge thinks it pushed off something. Did it?

▶ Premieres Tue Nov 17 Subscribe

Where this answer comes from

Each source below supports one claim in the episode. Links open the original pages.

  1. Higurashi et al., The Anatomical Record (2026): in cat spines the chest (thoracic) part twists easily and the lower back (lumbar) is stiffer. Filming of live cats showed the front trunk finishing its rotation before the back trunk, and the authors suggest the spine suits this.

    doi.org ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  2. Yamaguchi University English press release (2026-04-30): the university's own summary of the study, finding the thoracic vertebrae have a very high capacity for twisting.

    ds0n.cc.yamaguchi-u.ac.jp ↗

  3. BBC Science Focus, by physicist Gregory J. Gbur: Marey's 1894 photographs showed a cat starting with no spin and still turning over. In the bend-and-twist model the front and rear halves twist in opposite directions and their twists largely cancel.

    sciencefocus.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  4. Discover Magazine (2023), quoting physicist Greg Gbur: the cat rotates its front and back halves in opposite directions so the spins cancel. Early rigid-body analysis failed because cats are supple.

    discovermagazine.com ↗ · Archived copy ↗

  5. UNC Charlotte College of Science news (2026-03-09): front and back halves turn in opposite directions. The 2026 study footage showed the front half finishing its rotation before the rear, which Gbur says gives the tuck-and-turn model a little more credence.

    science.charlotte.edu ↗

  6. Nautilus (2026): the front of the body rotates first and the rear follows. The thoracic spine is very flexible while the lumbar spine is relatively rigid.

    nautil.us ↗ · Archived copy ↗
