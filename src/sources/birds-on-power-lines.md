# Sources: Why don't birds get shocked on power lines?

- https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/why-dont-birds-get-electrocuted-while-perching-on-power-lines/
  BBC Science Focus, physicist Robert Matthews of Aston University: it is voltage differences, not voltages themselves, that make electricity flow. A bird on a single line has no voltage difference, but touching another line at a different voltage would electrocute it, which is why companies space cables apart.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260713121114/https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/why-dont-birds-get-electrocuted-while-perching-on-power-lines
- https://engineering.mit.edu/ask-an-engineer/how-do-birds-sit-on-high-voltage-power-lines-without-getting-electrocuted
  MIT School of Engineering "Ask an Engineer", an independent cross-check: on a single wire both feet are at the same electrical potential, so current does not pass through the bird. The danger comes from touching a second wire at a different potential, or the wire plus the grounded pole.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260309084240/https://engineering.mit.edu/ask-an-engineer/how-do-birds-sit-on-high-voltage-power-lines-without-getting-electrocuted
- https://www.indianaconnection.org/birds-on-a-wire/
  Indiana Connection, an electric co-op magazine (2017), a third cross-check: there is no voltage difference in a single wire, and a bird is shocked if its body links two points at different potentials, such as the ground or a second wire.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260412053658/https://www.indianaconnection.org/birds-on-a-wire/
- https://www.usgs.gov/publications/electrocution
  USGS National Wildlife Health Center (N. J. Thomas, 1999): birds are electrocuted when they bridge two energized parts, or an energized part and a grounded metal part, most commonly where conductors are closer together than the wingspan. Raptors are especially at risk.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260207202053/https://www.usgs.gov/publications/electrocution
- https://www.fws.gov/story/avoidance-and-minimization-measures-power-lines
  U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service: electrocution happens when a bird touches two energized parts or an energized part and a grounded part. Older poles had narrow clearances, and avian-safe poles provide enough separation and cover exposed parts.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260830115828/https://www.fws.gov/story/avoidance-and-minimization-measures-power-lines
- https://eagles.org/take-action/avian-friendly-power-lines/
  American Eagle Foundation, citing APLIC: most electrocutions are on medium-voltage distribution lines where a wingspan can bridge the spacing. Fixes include wider spacing, longer crossarms, and covering conductors and equipment.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260616091717/https://eagles.org/take-action/avian-friendly-power-lines/
- https://www.aplic.org/
  Avian Power Line Interaction Committee, a utility-industry group that works with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and others on bird protection. Context only.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20261013224018/https://www.aplic.org/
