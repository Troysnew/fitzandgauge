# Sources: How do trains turn without a steering wheel?

- https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/30630/1/IMechE_Annual_Lectiure_Dec_2016.pdf
  Paul Allen and Philip Shackleton, "Wheels v Rails", IMechE Railway Division lecture (2016), University of Huddersfield Institute of Railway Research: a coned wheelset that shifts sideways rolls on two different radii, which lets it roll round a curve. It also notes that in reality perfect pure-rolling curving does not quite happen, and slip grows as curves get tighter.
- https://www.stle.org/files/TLTArchives/2024/08_August/Automotive_Tribology.aspx
  Dr. Edward Becker, Tribology & Lubrication Technology, Society of Tribologists and Lubrication Engineers (2024): two wheels on a solid axle turn at the same speed, and on a curve the outer wheel must travel farther. Conical wheels let the outer wheel move outward onto a larger diameter, while the flange mainly keeps the train on the track.
- https://www.thenakedscientists.com/get-naked/experiments/train-tracks-how-trains-go-round-corners
  The Naked Scientists, University of Cambridge-based science programme (2012): wheels are solidly attached to their axles and the outer rail is longer, so coned wheels the right way round make the train go round the corner on its own. On curves too sharp for the cone, the flanges rub, but most track doesn't corner that sharply.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20250119013246/https://www.thenakedscientists.com/get-naked/experiments/train-tracks-how-trains-go-round-corners
- https://practical.engineering/blog/2023/10/3/why-are-rails-shaped-like-that
  Wesley Crump, Practical Engineering (2023): wheels are conical, wider next to the flange and narrowing toward the outside, and a solid axle means both wheels turn at the same rate. In a curve the outer wheel rides on the thicker part of its tread, and flanges are there to keep a train from derailing.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20251009041341/https://practical.engineering/blog/2023/10/3/why-are-rails-shaped-like-that
- https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/train-wheel-science/
  Science Buddies and Svenja Lohner, Scientific American (2019): train wheels are slightly conical and the outside of a curve is slightly longer, so the outside wheel can travel farther while turning at the same rate. The flange prevents the train from falling off the tracks.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260206214225/https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/train-wheel-science/
