# Sources: Case Files: Why Did Chicago Lift Its Streets and Buildings?

- http://nike-of-samothrace.net/csc.html
  "The lifting of Chicago: sources", transcriptions of the Chicago Tribune and Press and Tribune of 1860-61 and other period accounts, on a private enthusiast's site: the Tremont House lift (about 5,000 screws, 500 men, a six-foot target, one foot on day one, the hotel staying open), the 1860 Lake Street block (about 6,000 screws, 600 men, quarter turns on a signal, 4 feet 8 inches in five days), and George Pullman's firm. Nearly every building-raising figure comes from this one paper, so the episode credits them to the Tribune.
  Archive: http://web.archive.org/web/20260617004540/https://www.nike-of-samothrace.net/csc.html
- http://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/1324.html
  Louis P. Cain, Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Water", read in an Internet Archive copy: the flat, low, muddy city, gravity sewers, raising the streets, the river reversal (including the "quietly" removed dam in January 1900), St. Louis's fears and the court case, the sharp drop in typhoid, and storms that could still force the gates open. Its account dates from about 2004, so the episode puts that last point in the past tense.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20231208155740/http://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/1324.html

  Louis P. Cain, Encyclopedia of Chicago, "Sanitation in Chicago", read in an Internet Archive copy: Chicago as the first large American city to build a comprehensive sewer system, the street raising, the lake tunnel, the canal, and chlorine from 1916. It is by the same author as "Water", so the two count as one voice.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20241227073147/https://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/300017.html

- https://www.straightdope.com/21343689/did-90-000-people-die-of-typhoid-fever-and-cholera-in-chicago-in-1885
  Cecil Adams, The Straight Dope, built on river historian Libby Hill's research: the real Aug 2, 1885 storm, about five and a half inches of rain in 19 hours, no cholera deaths reported that year, and typhoid only slightly above average. It debunks the myth that 90,000 people died, which nearly every debunking traces to Hill, so the episode credits her.
