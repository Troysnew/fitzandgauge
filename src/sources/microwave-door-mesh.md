# Sources: Why does a microwave door have a metal screen with tiny holes?

- https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Waves/mwoven.html
  HyperPhysics, Department of Physics and Astronomy, Georgia State University (C. R. Nave): microwave ovens run at 2,450 MHz, a wavelength of about 12 cm. The holes in the door's metal screen are about 1 mm across, so the microwaves are effectively blocked, while visible light waves are far smaller than the holes and pass through.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20261018153829/http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Waves/mwoven.html
- https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12217
  University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign Department of Physics, Ask the Van (2008): most ovens use 2.45 GHz microwaves, a wavelength of about 12 centimeters. A thin metal screen with millimeter-size holes is an effective shield because the big waves can't get through the small holes, and microwaves pass easily through glass.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260612201211/https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/12217
- https://howeverythingworks.org/?p=811
  Lou Bloomfield, How Everything Works (1996): the metal grid on the oven door reflects microwaves and keeps them inside, because microwaves can't pass through holes much smaller than their wavelength, while light has no trouble passing through.
- https://howeverythingworks.org/?p=1829
  Lou Bloomfield, How Everything Works (2009): currents in the metal re-radiate the microwaves in the reflected direction, so the mesh acts like a metal surface, because the holes are much smaller than the 12.2-cm wavelength.
- https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens
  U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Microwave Ovens (2023): microwaves are reflected by metal and pass through glass, paper, plastic and similar materials.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20261018155114/https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens
