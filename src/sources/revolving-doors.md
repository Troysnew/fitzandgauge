# Sources: Why do tall buildings have revolving doors?

- https://www.slate.com/articles/health_and_science/the_green_lantern/2008/07/are_revolving_doors_more_energy_efficient.html
  Slate, "The Green Lantern" (2008, with a July 29, 2008 correction): in winter heated air rises and cold air enters at ground level. A revolving door is not airtight but reduces the pressure-difference effect; the MIT student study of Building E25 found 23% of visitors used the revolving doors and that a swing door let in up to eight times more air.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20181220233338/https://www.slate.com:80/articles/health_and_science/the_green_lantern/2008/07/are_revolving_doors_more_energy_efficient.html
- https://news.mit.edu/2009/revolve-0403
  MIT News (April 3, 2009): MIT students' research indicates that if everyone used the revolving doors in Building E25, MIT would save almost $7,500 in natural gas a year and nearly 15 tons of CO2.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20240728071726/https://news.mit.edu/2009/revolve-0403
- https://www.csemag.com/seven-ways-to-fight-stack-effect-in-buildings/
  Consulting-Specifying Engineer (2020), by a mechanical engineer: warm air rises and the lowest floors become negatively pressurized, sucking in cold air. Revolving doors achieve the same result as vestibules, limiting the cold air to a small puff.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260421034545/https://www.csemag.com/seven-ways-to-fight-stack-effect-in-buildings/
- https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/the-stack-effect-when-buildings-act-like-chimneys
  GreenBuildingAdvisor (2010), quoting building scientist John Straube: warm air rises, so the top of a heated building is pressurized and the bottom depressurized. Early skyscrapers needed revolving doors because the front door could not be opened against the stack effect pressure.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20220924164318/https://www.greenbuildingadvisor.com/article/the-stack-effect-when-buildings-act-like-chimneys
- https://www.proremodeler.com/understanding-stack-effect
  Pro Remodeler (2017, no named author, used as a cross-check): the effect grows with building height and temperature difference, and a normal door at the base of a very tall building can be very hard to open. Many tall buildings have revolving doors at ground level.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20240613211332/https://www.proremodeler.com/understanding-stack-effect
- https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/quantitative-reduction-method-of-draft-in-high-rise-buildings-usi/
  Lee et al. (2012), Indoor and Built Environment, peer-reviewed abstract: stack-effect drafts in high-rise buildings are a known problem, and revolving doors are commonly used to reduce them, mainly at the lobby entrance.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260614170005/https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/quantitative-reduction-method-of-draft-in-high-rise-buildings-usi/
- https://www.boonedam.com/en-us/mit-study-finds-revolving-doors-reduce-air-infiltration-compared-swinging-doors
  Boon Edam, a revolving-door manufacturer (interested party, cross-check only): reports the 2006 MIT study in E25 with 23% usage, a swing door letting through as much as 8 times more air, and almost $7,500 in natural gas a year.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20250517175441/https://www.boonedam.com/en-us/mit-study-finds-revolving-doors-reduce-air-infiltration-compared-swinging-doors
