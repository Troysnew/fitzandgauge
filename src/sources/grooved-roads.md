# Sources: Why do some roads have grooves cut into them?

- https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/14065/003.cfm
  Federal Highway Administration, Evaluation of Pavement Safety Performance (2015): grooving's primary objective is to improve drainage and mitigate hydroplaning. Several studies show grooved pavements reduce wet-weather crashes, with a caution that they did not control for regression to the mean.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260309214135/https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/14065/003.cfm
- https://www.txdot.gov/manuals/des/hyd/chapter-10--storm-drains/section-4--pavement-drainage/hydroplaning.html
  Texas Department of Transportation Hydraulic Design Manual: hydroplaning occurs when the drainage capacity of the tire tread and pavement surface is exceeded. Grooving may be a corrective measure for severe localized hydroplaning problems.
- https://wwwapps.dotd.la.gov/administration/dotdaz/definition.aspx?termID=374
  Louisiana DOTD glossary: grooving cuts slots into a pavement surface to provide channels for water to escape beneath tires, improving skid resistance and reducing the potential for hydroplaning.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20260614062506/https://wwwapps.dotd.la.gov/administration/dotdaz/definition.aspx?termID=374
- https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/legsregs/directives/techadvs/t514010.htm
  FHWA Technical Advisory T 5140.10 (1979, canceled 2005, so historical): both types of grooving effectively reduce hydroplaning potential, and longitudinal grooving is an effective means of reducing accidents at sites with high wet-weather accident rates. Wider, deeper grooves can be expected to increase the absolute noise level by a significant amount.
  Archive: https://web.archive.org/web/20250502042158/https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/legsregs/directives/techadvs/t514010.htm
